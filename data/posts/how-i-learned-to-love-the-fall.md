***I. Two Trees***

Two trees greeted me on my morning walk to the dining hall. The first stood tall and sported a lush crown of gold foliage. The second sat a little further around the bend. It was half the other’s height, and tattered brown leaves peeled off its branches. Its rusting leaves were so sparse I could stick my hand through them, a pile already gathering at its base. Only one seemed worth photographing. I took a picture of both.

Fall has always been my favorite season for superficial reasons. At first, it was the weather that drew me. Unlike most California residents, I harbor a secret grudge against the warmer months. Spring allergies meant a clogged nose as I ran down soccer fields in washed-out jerseys three times my size. I remember suffocating in our oven of a Honda Civic, unwilling to open a window and let the hot summer air in. Out-of-state readers may think I was spoiled by the Californian climate. Perhaps. I suspect I have an undiagnosed hypersensitivity to the sun. I’ve also fully bought into the image of fall that culture sells us: auburn trees, apple cider, the fire crackling as Charlie Brown re-runs play on TV.

So when September comes and the air turns cold, I feel refreshed in body and soul. The world takes on a new appearance. It isn’t just the changing leaves, but the crisp atmosphere of chilly days, of a pleasant mild grey settling onto the world around me.

For a long time, I could only explain my love for autumn as a preference for mood. Lately, I suspect it’s something deeper.

My life was structured into seasons for the first time when I went from homeschooling to college. I hear public schools partition time with intentional breaks and periods. Not for me. I lived in a freeform fusion of school, chores, and rest three-hundred-and-sixty-five days a year. I’d finish homework at dinner, read for hours on a Wednesday, and take classes into the summer. In college, midterms and semesters divided time into literal and metaphorical seasons.

I find my fondest memories in the chill of autumn. Not necessarily my happiest. My fondest. In the fall of 2023, I traded routine hangouts with hometown friends for excursions with strangers. One November night, my new friends and I spent the evening at a bowling alley away from school. Caught up in the excitement of bowling a triple strike in the last frame, we missed our Uber and wandered back through the below sixty-degree night on foot. We walked under dim yellow streetlights as beat-up Camrys sped down the Pomona streets, arguing about nothing and creating jokes we still use today. I thought of my 16th birthday, when my childhood friends surprised me at the local bowling alley.

Each fall brings the excitement of new things as the old ones slip away. There’s a shared wistfulness in the autumn-inspired art that I love. Take for example the jazz standard Autumn Leaves, with lyrics adapted by Johnny Mercer:

>> The falling leaves drift by the window  
>> The autumn leaves of red and gold  
>> I see your lips, the summer kisses  
>> The sun-burned hands I used to hold

Mercer finds beauty in memory and tragedy in impermanence. I’m particularly fond of the Bill Evans rendition. Evans’ lyrical phrasings dance lightly around an upbeat swing even as we’re constantly pulled back into a nostalgic G-minor.

I really started noticing this after watching Peter Yates’ 1973 neo-noir *The Friends of Eddie Coyle*. Eddie, an aging hood trying to avoid prison by snitching on his crew, meanders through autumn in Boston. The film has an incredible russet palette: cozy tan wool sweaters, burnt corduroy jackets, mahogany suburban interiors, and a gorgeous smattering of dissipating leaves. There’s a scene where Eddie, played by aging star Robert Mitchum, unaware he’s about to die, watches a young Bobby Orr at a Bruins game. “What is he, 24 or something? Greatest hockey player in the world. Geeze, *what a future he's got*, huh?”
