*“He hath made everything beautiful in its time.”*  
*— Ecclesiastes 3:11 (KJV)*

***I. Two Trees***

Two trees greeted me on my morning walk to the dining hall. The first stood tall and sported a lush crown of gold foliage. The second sat a little further around the bend. It was half the other’s height, and tattered brown leaves peeled off its branches. Its rusting leaves were so sparse I could stick my hand through them, a pile already gathering at its base. Only one seemed worth photographing. I took a picture of both.

Fall has always been my favorite season for superficial reasons. I’ve fully bought into the image of fall that culture sells: auburn trees, apple cider, the fire crackling as Charlie Brown re-runs play on TV. More simply, it’s the weather. Unlike most California residents, I harbor a secret grudge against the warmer months. With summer came a sun too bright, blinding me as I ran down soccer fields in washed-out jerseys three times my size. I remember suffocating in our oven of a Honda Civic, unwilling to open a window and let the hot air in. Out-of-state readers may think I was spoiled by the Californian climate. Perhaps. I suspect I have an undiagnosed hypersensitivity to the sun. 

So when September comes and the air turns cold, I feel refreshed. I relish the sensations of crisp chilly days, and my eyes rest easy on the pleasant grey of the world around me. My favorite moments in college have been feeling the night wind in my hair as I skateboard down the grassy lane to my dorm, leaves crunching under rubber wheels. For a long time, I could only explain my love for autumn as a preference for mood. The real reason may be deeper.

My life was structured into seasons for the first time when I went from homeschooling to college. I hear public schools partition time with intentional breaks and periods. Not for the homeschooler, who lives in a freeform fusion of school, chores, and rest three-hundred-and-sixty-five days a year. I’d finish homework at dinner, read for hours on a Wednesday, and take classes into the summer. You can’t close chapters in your life if there are none to close. In college, midterms, breaks, and semesters divided time into literal and metaphorical seasons, and I felt the odd experience of something coming to an end. I roamed campus the week before that first winter break, saying goodbye to everyone from my roommate to the late-night cleaning staff.

I find my fondest memories in the chill of autumn. Not necessarily my happiest, but my fondest. In the fall of 2023, I traded routine hangouts with hometown friends for excursions with strangers. One November night, my new friends and I spent the evening at a bowling alley away from school. Caught up in the excitement of bowling a triple strike in the last frame, we missed our Uber and wandered back through the below sixty-degree night on foot. We walked under dim yellow streetlights as beat-up Camrys sped down the Pomona streets, arguing about nothing and creating jokes we still use today. I thought of my 16th birthday, when my childhood friends surprised me at the local bowling alley. That night, we had shared a cake in the pizza parlor next door, days before lockdown started. I can’t remember the last time we were all in the same room.

Each fall brings the excitement of new things as the old ones slip away. There’s a shared wistfulness in the autumn-inspired art that I love. Take for example the jazz standard Autumn Leaves, with lyrics adapted by Johnny Mercer:

>The falling leaves drift by the window  
>The autumn leaves of red and gold  
>I see your lips, the summer kisses  
>The sun-burned hands I used to hold

Mercer finds beauty in memory and tragedy in impermanence. I’m particularly fond of the Bill Evans rendition, where his lyrical phrasings dance lightly around an upbeat swing even as we’re constantly pulled back into a nostalgic G-minor.

(first started thinking about autumn’s transitory nature) I watched Peter Yates’ 1973 neo-noir *The Friends of Eddie Coyle*. A late-career Robert Mitchum plays Eddie, an aging hood trying to avoid prison by snitching on his crew. The film has an incredible russet palette: burnt corduroy jackets, mahogany suburban interiors, and a gorgeous smattering of dissipating leaves. There’s a scene where Eddie, unaware that he’s about to die, watches a young Bobby Orr at a Bruins game: “What is he, 24 or something? Greatest hockey player in the world. Geeze, *what a future he's got, huh?*” 

***II. Goldengrove Unleaving***

The Victorian poets took fall, loss, and beauty and tied them to time. Gerard Manley Hopkins struggled with a lifelong conflict between his religious devotion, ascetic guilt, and creativity. Upon becoming a Jesuit, he burned all his early poems. Hopkins, one of my favorite poets, wrote about autumn and loss in his poem *Spring and Fall:* 

>Márgarét, áre you gríeving  
>Over Goldengrove unleaving?

>…By and by, nor spare a sigh  
>Though worlds of wanwood leafmeal lie;

>…It ís the blight man was born for,  
>It is Margaret you mourn for.

Hopkins interprets a child’s grief over shedding leaves as mourning for man’s mortality. While nature’s cyclical promise is implied, the hope of return is reserved for the fall foliage. Our condition is permanent: we are dust, and to dust we shall return.

Like Hopkins, Victorian contemporary Christina Rossetti struggled with depression and religious renunciation, breaking off two engagements for her faith. Rossetti took a more hopeful turn in the first two stanzas of *A Better Resurrection*. 

>I have no wit, no words, no tears;  
>My heart within me like a stone  
>Is numb'd too much for hopes or fears;  
>Look right, look left, I dwell alone;  
>I lift mine eyes, but dimm'd with grief  
>No everlasting hills I see;  
>My life is in the falling leaf:  
>O Jesus, quicken me.

>…My life is like a frozen thing,  
>No bud nor greenness can I see:  
>*Yet rise it shall—the sap of Spring;*  
>*O Jesus, rise in me.*

Unlike Hopkins, Rossetti extends the hope of seasonal renewal to her own life. Just as falling leaves precede spring blooms, she will rise from the barren dusk.

Two nights ago, I made the long walk back from choir practice to my dorm. It was a quiet night, and I could feel the whistling wind tingling on my skin. I pulled my jacket tight around me, but I couldn’t shake the chill. Almost by habit, I took a detour, lingering by places that used to feel like home. But there was nowhere to go but back. I lay awake in bed, staring at a tea-cup, at giant cardboard cookies, and old receipts. I felt my my heart within me like a stone.

With nothing else left, I began flipping through poems and prose from Hopkins and Rossetti. It seemed as if over a century ago, they had sat outside on a cold November night and felt what I felt. I found, like James Baldwin said, "the things that tormented me most were the very things that connected me with all the people… who had ever been alive.” Our lives were in the falling leaf.

What Hopkins and Rossetti never address is that while the trees have an assurance of *re-leaving, we don’t*. Nature's seasons are in a rhythm. We don’t know when our leaves come and go, when our spring ends and the fall comes. Like Eddie Coyle, all we imagine is the future, blind to the loss that could happen. For twenty years, this unknowability stopped me from fully investing in anything. In the fall of 2026, I learned to embrace the new. As it ends, I’m learning to let it go.

It’s the unknowability that makes every mood feel more sharp and real. As the air gets crisper, every moment is more sharp, wistful, and beautiful in its transcience. \[a specific detail?\] I still love the fall, but not just because of the fresh air and cozy sweaters. I’m learning to embrace the loss of the old and promise of the new,in all its unknowable transcience, and see the great beauty in botih. (Feel like ive worded this better always)

I walk by the two trees every morning. The gold leaves of the first one are starting to droop and fall one by one, floating away with each gust of wind. And yesterday I noticed a tawny gradient, a pied beauty in the brown leaves of its aged friend.
