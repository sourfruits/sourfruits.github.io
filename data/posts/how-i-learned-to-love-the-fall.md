*“He hath made every thing beautiful in his time.”*  
*— Ecclesiastes 3:11 (KJV)*

***I. Two Trees***

Two trees greeted me on my morning walk to the dining hall. The first stood tall and sported a lush crown of gold foliage. The second sat a little further down the bend. It was half the other’s height, and tattered brown leaves peeled off its branches. Its rusting leaves were so sparse I could stick my hand through them, a pile already gathering at its base. Only one seemed worth photographing. I took a picture of both.

Fall has always been my favorite season for superficial reasons. Unlike most California residents, I harbor a secret grudge against the warmer months. With summer came a sun too bright, blinding me as I ran down soccer fields in jerseys three times my size. I remember suffocating in our oven of a Honda Civic, unwilling to let the hot air in. Out-of-state readers may think I was spoiled by the Californian climate. I suspect I have an undiagnosed hypersensitivity to the sun. 

So when September comes and the air turns cold, I feel refreshed. I relish the sensations of crisp chilly days, and my eyes rest easy on the pleasant grey of the world around me. My favorite moments in college have been feeling the night wind in my hair as I skateboard down the grassy lane to my dorm, leaves crunching under rubber wheels. For a long time, I could only explain my love for autumn as a preference for mood.

My life was structured into seasons for the first time when I went from homeschooling to college. I hear public schools partition time with intentional breaks and periods. Not for the homeschooler, who lives in a freeform fusion of school, chores, and rest three-hundred-and-sixty-five days a year. I’d finish homework at dinner, read for hours on a Wednesday, and take classes into the summer. There are no chapters in life if the first one never ends. In college, midterms, breaks, and semesters divided time into literal and metaphorical seasons, and I experienced the odd feeling of something coming to an end. The week before that first winter break, I roamed campus, saying goodbye to everyone from my roommate to the late-night cleaning staff.

I find my fondest memories lie in the chill of autumn. Not necessarily my happiest, but my fondest. In the fall of 2023, I traded routine hangouts with hometown friends for excursions with strangers. One November night, my new friends and I spent the evening at a bowling alley away from school. Caught up in the excitement of bowling a triple strike in the last frame, we missed our Uber and wandered back through the below-sixty-degree night on foot. We walked under dim yellow streetlights as beat-up Camrys sped down the Pomona streets, arguing about nothing and creating jokes we still use today. I thought of my 16th birthday, when my childhood friends surprised me at the local bowling alley. That night, we had shared a cake in the pizza parlor next door, days before lockdown started. I can’t remember the last time we were all in the same room.

I started thinking about autumn and its transience after watching Peter Yates’ 1973 neo-noir *The Friends of Eddie Coyle*. A late-career Robert Mitchum plays Eddie, an aging small-time crook meandering through autumn in Boston. The film has a gorgeous palette: burnt corduroy jackets, mahogany suburban interiors, and dissipating russet leaves. There’s a scene where Eddie, unaware that he’s about to die, watches a young Bobby Orr at a Bruins game: “What is he, 24 or something? Greatest hockey player in the world. Geeze, what a future he's got, huh?” 

***II. Goldengrove Unleaving***

Two nights ago, I made the long walk from choir practice back to my dorm. Like always, I was the first one out of the building. Usually, I leave in anticipation; this time I just wanted to be alone. I pulled my jacket tight around me, but I couldn’t shake the chill. Almost by habit, I took a detour past the campus cafe, past places that used to feel like home. That night, I lay awake in bed, staring at a teacup, at old receipts and giant cardboard cookies. I began flipping through poems from Hopkins and Rossetti, two poets who spent their lives choosing between faith and their own desires. 

Gerard Manley Hopkins’ life and work were marked by melancholy. Torn between ascetic self-denial and creative passion, he burned his early poems upon entering the Jesuit order. Hopkins writes in *Spring and Fall:* 

>Márgarét, áre you gríeving  
>Over Goldengrove unleaving?

>…By and by, nor spare a sigh  
>Though worlds of wanwood leafmeal lie;

>…It ís the blight man was born for,  
>It is Margaret you mourn for.

In the poem, a child grieving the shedding of leaves is really mourning for man’s mortality. The Goldengrove will return, but our condition is permanent: we are dust, and to dust we shall return.

Christina Rossetti, who greatly influenced Hopkins, struggled with the grief of renunciation, breaking off two engagements for her faith. She takes a more hopeful turn in the first two stanzas of *A Better Resurrection*. 

>I have no wit, no words, no tears;  
>My heart within me like a stone  
>Is numb'd too much for hopes or fears;  
>Look right, look left, I dwell alone;  
>I lift mine eyes, but dimm'd with grief  
>No everlasting hills I see;  
>My life is in the falling leaf:  
>O Jesus, quicken me.

>…Yet rise it shall—the sap of Spring;  
>O Jesus, rise in me.

Unlike Hopkins, Rossetti gives herself the hope of seasonal renewal. Just as falling leaves come before spring blooms, she will rise from “the barren dusk.” It seemed as if over a century ago, these two had sat outside on a cold November night and felt what I felt.

What the poets never answer is that while the trees know the timing of their re-leaving, we don’t. We don’t know when our leaves come and go, when our fall ends and our winter comes. Like Eddie Coyle, all we imagine is the future, blind to loss that could happen. For years, this unknowability made me hesitant to fully invest myself. In the fall of 2026, I learned to embrace the new. As it ends, I’m trying to let it go. Every night, I find myself reaching for my phone to send a song or a poem, then stopping myself.

I could say that there’s comfort in knowing that to every thing there is a season, that God makes every thing beautiful in his time. But right now I couldn’t care less. I hate that I have to see the leaves fall. I hate that I can’t control, or even know the timing of my seasons. Even knowing that my autumn could end at any moment, I feel no regret for having chosen it. I’d do it all again.

I walk by the two trees every morning. The gold leaves of the first one are starting to droop and fall one by one, floating away with each gust of wind. And yesterday I noticed a tawny gradient, a pied beauty in the brown leaves of its aged friend.
