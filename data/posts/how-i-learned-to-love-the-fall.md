Fall has always been my favorite season for superficial reasons. My earliest explanation is that I was born with a temperament for crisp breezes and cloudy skies. I’ve also fully bought into the image that culture sells us: auburn trees, apple cider, pumpkin pie, the fire crackling as Charlie Brown re-runs play on TV. For a long time, I could only explain it as a preference for mood. Lately, I suspect it’s something deeper.

At first, it was the weather that drew me to fall. Unlike most California residents, I harbor a secret grudge against the warmer months. Spring meant allergies clogging up my nose as I ran up and down the soccer field in washed-out jerseys three times my size. With summer came a sun too bright and enervating, sapping my energy as I write in my room. I remember suffocating in our oven of a Honda Civic, unwilling to open a window and let the hot air in. Out-of-state readers may think I was spoiled by the Californian climate. Perhaps. I suspect I have an undiagnosed hypersensitivity to the sun. So when September comes and the air turns cold, I feel my soul refreshed anew. The world takes on a new appearance. It isn’t just the changing leaves, it's the crisp feel of chilly days, of a pleasant mild grey settling onto the world around me.

Life was structured into seasons for the first time when I went from homeschooling to college. Public and private schools, from what I’m told, have intentional “periods” and “breaks” that partition the school year. Not for me. I lived in a freeform flow of class, work, and play three-hundred-and-sixty-five days a year. In college, midterms and semesters divided my temporal experience into literal and metaphorical seasons for the first time. 

* Reflecting on these seasons, I find my fondest memories occurring in the melancholy chill of autumn.   
* Each fall move-in has brought something new: my initial encounter with campus, reuniting with newly "old" friends for the first time, kindling relationships, etc.   
* At the same time, every August is \_\_\_\_ by the feeling that I’m leaving something behind. Have you ever been able to see a feeling? I have. Every fall, I feel this reinvention of self as something I can feel and see in the air, leaves, sky around me. As the trees shed parts of themselves, so do I. IT is a wistfulness Reinforced by the crisp chill I love so much. My Autumn is characterized by discovery, by new things, but also the loss of the old. 

There’s a universality to the wistfulness I feel. All autumn-inspired art I encounter is tinged with this simultaneously melancholy and beauty. They can’t seem to avoid either the beauty of the season or its transience. Take for example the jazz standard Autumn Leaves, with lyrics translated from a French poem by Johnny Mercer:

> The falling leaves drift by the window  
> The autumn leaves of red and gold  
> I see your lips, the summer kisses  
> The sun-burned hands I used to hold

Even as Mercer finds beauty in memory, his description of drifting leaves evokes the tragedy of impermanence. Sonically, the music does the same. I’m particularly fond of the Bill Evans rendition, which takes on a heavy upbeat swing. Evans’ lyrical phrasings dance lightly around Eddie Gomez’s airy bass, but we’re reminded of the underlying wistfulness as they’re oft pulled back into G-minor.

Peter Yates' 1973 neo-noir *The Friends of Eddie Coyle* takes autumn’s melancholy to the extreme. *Eddie Coyle* stars an older Robert Mitchum as the titular character, an aging hood trying to escape a prison sentence by snitching on former accomplices. I watched the film following Mitchum *Out of the Past*, but quickly realized that *Eddie Coyle* is a quintessential fall film. It has an incredible russet palette: cozy tan wool sweaters, burnt corduroy jackets, mahogany suburban interiors, and a gorgeous smattering of dissipating leaves.
