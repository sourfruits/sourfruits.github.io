The strangest scene in *Primetime* is not the hallucinatory demon vision. Somewhere around the 45 minute mark, Robert Pattinson’s Chris Hansen drags his kids out of bed to watch the latest successful taping of *To Catch a Predator*. His sons sit slack-jawed in the middle of the night as their father gloats over his new Wednesday night time slot. *Daddy’s gonna be on primetime,* he seems to say, *that’s when the most people are watching\!* His kids reply with an indifferent “that’s cool.” Hansen constantly refers to his show as a righteous crusade against an evil force, but interactions like these reveal the dissonance between Hansen’s stated purpose and his warped desire for numeric validation. His identity is derived from his work, but his work is based on popularity, spectacle, and network approval.

For a film marketed around Pattinson’s portrayal of showrunner Chris Hansen, there is surprising screentime dedicated to producer Andie Bender and decoy Dan Plumb (played by Jimmy Olsen actor Skyler Gisondo). Like Hansen, these characters struggle compellingly with a career-defined identity warped by the pressure of the entertainment industry. Bender, channeling the wanton heartlessness of Faye Dunaway’s Diana in *Network*, is completely consumed by her career — her last thought after Conradt's suicide is that she can "save this." Plumb places as much stock in his job as an actor, seeking accomplishment and pride in expertise even as it alienates him and brings him to despair by the film’s ending. 

Beyond its characters, a big part of *Primetime’s* appeal is in its unique stylization. Oppenheim lends his documentary experience to creative, impeccable shot composition. The film lingers on voyeuristic close-ups of its characters framed by doorways, reflected in mirrors, and washes it all in a smoldering blue. Direct camcorder shots (screens are always REC-ording) are mixed among modern digital footage. The characters are overdramatic caricatures — Pattinson plays Hansen with intensity, dressing himself in a constructed, practiced, and gratingly inauthentic persona. In his first appearance, he recites lines back to himself before entering the set with a literal flourish through the curtains. What a diva\! *Primetime* watches less like a gritty biopic than a maddeningly entertaining fever dream.

I don’t think *Primetime* has much to offer in the way of commentary on *Predator*, or even generally on voyeurism-as-entertainment. It is, however, a gorgeous and bleakly humorous delirium accentuated by a light dose of 2000s nostalgia (who remembers *What Would You Do?*) and Pattinson’s uncanny character acting.
