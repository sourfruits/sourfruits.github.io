*Originally written for Letterboxd, updated 10/3*

I’ve always felt a connection to Bruce Lee. I’m the son of immigrant parents; my mom spent her early years in 1970s Hong Kong, and my dad grew up in San Francisco’s Chinatown. Lee, having been raised in both, embodied those two worlds. The family connection runs deeper — my great uncle trained with Lee’s mentor Ip Man in Hong Kong. This overlap was reinforced by constant comparisons to the martial arts legend when I was younger. At age 6, I rocked a nasty bowl cut and owned a yellow tracksuit that looked identical to Lee’s jumpsuit in *Game of Death*. I remember strangers (including a hotel attendant in Peru who spoke no English) often commenting that I looked like Lee.

For as long as I can remember, Bruce Lee has felt like a distant relative. My first encounter with his filmography did not disappoint. *Enter the Dragon* is 100 minutes of genre-blending fun that captures a unique personal slice of history.

On the surface, *Enter the Dragon* is part Bond thriller and part Blaxploitation flick, all adorned in the garb of Hong Kong cinema. The film features martial arts and espouses Confucian values of family and honor. It even features an Asian man as the apex force in a film with Western actors. However, what makes this film special is its depiction of a Hong Kong now lost to time. My mom, who grew up there in the 70s, would regale me with tales of the magic of Hong Kong under British rule. To her, it was an era of shared respect and cultural integration.

In the movie, British intelligence and Shaolin masters work with mutual respect. The scenes in the harbor show the visual dichotomy of a modernizing Hong Kong. Sampan rowers in baggy work jackets ferry passengers to and from larger junk vessels. Western-style skyscrapers loom in the background, indicative of the skyline to come. Bruce Lee’s character straddles cultures in his fashion choices, alternating between slick tailored Western suits and traditional kung fu garb. 

70s HK is a time I will never fully experience, and can only catch glimpses of. *Enter the Dragon* is pulpy exploitation that gives life to the world of my mother’s stories.
