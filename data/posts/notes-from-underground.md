When reading Notes from Underground, I was struck by how Dostoevsky knew human ugliness so intimately. Perhaps That shouldn't be surprising. Notes from Underground was Dostoevsky’s first novella following his last-minute reprieve from execution by firing squad. His survival, and subsequent exile in Siberia, deeply transformed his worldview, shifting his work towards darker themes of morality and suffering. connection should be that it takes up pre-exile themes  but dials up the disgusting huan nature to a thousand.

The narrator — like in the pre-exile story White Nights — is a delusional self-sabotaging protagonist who cannot separate daydreams from reality. His madness bleeds through the page as he rambles, backtracks, contradicts himself. Yet while White Nights' dreamer is hapless and almost gentle, the narrator of Notes is angrier, more self-absorbed, and wholly unsympathetic. The Underground Man is a man of contradictions in a prison of his own accord. He’s simultaneously self-aware of his failings and frozen by his intellectual overthinking. He craves connection and acceptance while extolling himself above his peers, hating and loathing “quote about whatever like the ones who don’t.” What’s so compelling is that the root of his madness is a shared human desire for recognition that Dostoevsky understands, and has followed to its logical conclusion. 

There is a stark disparity between reality and the narrator’s vivid fantasies of significance, of which he is always the slighted and intellectual hero. At one point, he paces around a room for 30 minutes, an elaborate mental plot to get a rise out of schoolyard enemies, who in turn barely notice his presence. For weeks, he constructs elaborate fantasies to punish an officer over a perceived offense; his rival never gives him any thought. As the Underground Man sinks deeper into poverty and humiliation, he insists on this deep cognitive dissonance to avoid confronting the reality of his situation. It is only after a sparse moment of sentimentality with a prostitute, that he inadvertently allows the reality of who he is to bleed into the false identity he has constructed and projects onto others. Unwilling to accept the discomfort, he flees.

In a broader context, Notes from Underground is also interesting as an ideological refutation through fiction of 19th-century Russian nihilism and utopianism (it directly responds to Chernyshevsky's What Is to Be Done, which later inspired Lenin’s ideology). However, it stands out to me as a bleak, incisive study of the worst parts of man wrapped up in intoxicating, rambling prose. At each page, I was increasingly disgusted and yet could never put it down.
