When reading Notes from Underground, I was struck by how intimately Dostoevsky knew human ugliness. The Underground Man is absurdly, almost unbelievably pathetic. Still, he remains nuanced and strangely compelling in his downwards spiral.

This story’s narrator — like in the pre-exile White Nights — is a delusional self-sabotaging dreamer who cannot separate fantasy from reality. His madness bleeds through the page as he backtracks and contradicts himself. Yet while White Nights' dreamer is hapless and almost gentle in his fantasies, the narrator of Notes is angrier, self-absorbed, and wholly unsympathetic. On some days, he embarks on drunken tirades before begging for forgiveness. On others, he drives himself into debt to avenge perceived slights. He is paradoxical in his ravings, longing for connection while accosting others with misanthropic rage. 

Dostoevsky wrote his extremity as a response to Chernyshevsky's utilitarian novel What Is to Be Done (itself a response to the nihilism of Fathers and Sons). The Underground Man’s irrational behavior refutes the determinist idea that humans can act in perfect mathematical harmony. It’s an interesting intellectual back-and-forth uniquely told through fiction, but Notes from Underground stood out to me as an incisive study of the worst parts of man. Even as the Man's behavior becomes increasingly ludicrous, Dostoevsky's nuanced interior dialogue keeps his actions disturbingly understandable. At each page, I was increasingly disgusted (what do you mean you walked in a circle for 3 hours to get get noticed by your enemies) and yet could never put it down.
