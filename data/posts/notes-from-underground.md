When reading Notes from Underground, I was struck by how intimately Dostoevsky knew human ugliness. Perhaps it shouldn't be surprising. Notes from Underground was Dostoevsky’s first novella following his last-minute reprieve from execution by firing squad. His survival and subsequent exile in Siberia deeply transformed his worldview, shifting his work towards darker themes of morality and suffering.

The Underground Man is absurdly, almost unbelievably pathetic. This story’s narrator — like in the pre-exile White Nights — is a delusional self-sabotaging dreamer who cannot separate fantasy from reality. His madness bleeds through the page as he backtracks and contradicts himself. Yet while White Nights' dreamer is hapless and almost gentle in his fantasies, the narrator of Notes is angrier, self-absorbed, and wholly unsympathetic. On some days, he embarks on drunken tirades before begging for forgiveness. On others, he drives himself into debt to avenge perceived slights. He is paradoxical in his ravings, longing for connection while accosting others with misanthropic rage. Nevertheless, his desire to exist as an individual remains strangely compelling through his downward spiral.

In a broader context, Notes from Underground is also interesting as an ideological refutation through fiction of 19th-century Russian utilitarianism (the Man's irrational behavior challenges the utopian paradise in Chernyshevsky's novel What Is to Be Done). It’s an interesting intellectual back-and-forth told uniquely through fiction, but Notes from Underground stands out to me as an incisive study of the worst parts of man. Even as the Man's behavior becomes increasingly ludicrous, Dostoevsky's nuanced interior dialogue keeps his actions disturbingly understandable. At each page, I was increasingly disgusted (what do you mean you walked in a circle for 3 hours to get noticed by your enemies?) and yet could never put it down.
