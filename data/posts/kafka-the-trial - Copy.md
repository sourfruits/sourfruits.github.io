I started *The Trial* having consumed some of its inescapable influence through Bulgakov's *The Master and Margarita*, Gilliam's *Brazil*, and Scorsese's *After Hours* (all personal favorites), so I had the bleak humor of navigating an absurd bureaucratic nightmare in mind as I read.

What surprised me is how subtly funny the book is. It’s not overtly ludicrous like *The* *Master and Margarita* (there are no alcoholic pistol-toting cats), and Josef K. is more outwardly subdued than Griffin Dunne’s frantic Paul Hackett. Still, the way Kafka plays ridiculous moments straight brought me to tears on the BART at least once.

One of my favorite moments is when Kafka refuses to summarize the lawyer’s lengthy monologue on the inner workings of the trial, instead making us sit through ~10 pages of legal jargon. There’s no explicit humor in the lawyer’s ramblings, but when you flip the eighth page and he’s still blathering on about inane courtroom procedure, there’s nothing you can do but laugh. Kafka gives us the experience of tedium to show the innate hilarity in K’s powerlessness.

Anyways, I wasn’t surprised to learn that Kafka was often unable to finish reading chapter drafts to his friends because he would laugh too much. For someone who wrote so vividly about alienation and existential dread, he didn’t seem to take himself too seriously. I think it’s this sense of irreverence that makes The Trial so potent as an allegory for the absurdity of life.
